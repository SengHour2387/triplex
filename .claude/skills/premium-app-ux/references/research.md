# Research behind the framework

These findings come from Tim Gabe's lecture "The Psychology of Premium Apps" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8EAYZe6y3I8). Timestamps point to where each is discussed. When citing them to a user, attribute them to the lecture and its sources, and present figures as the lecture reports them rather than as independently verified facts. If the user needs a primary source for a stakeholder, suggest they follow the lecture's references or search for the original study.

## Foundations

**Pretty vs. premium (packaging studies, 2010 and 2021)** — [01:26]
Visual aesthetics get a product picked; tangible evidence of quality during use is what earns the "premium" label.

**Cognitive fluency** — [02:38]
Decades of research show that what's easy for the brain to process gets judged more favorably. Every pause to wonder "did that work?" adds processing effort and lowers the judgment.

**The commodity ceiling** — [00:20], [01:12]
With shared AI tools and pattern libraries, decent-looking UIs are cheap and look alike. AI improvements raise the floor for everyone at once, so they don't differentiate [03:29]. What stays yours is the work of thinking through every state.

## Pillar 1 — System states

**AI tool study (mid-2026)** — [02:03]–[02:17]
Five leading AI building tools were given identical design tasks. They explained their UX reasoning confidently, but roughly 25% of that reasoning never made it into the actual interface. Researchers called the gap "design theater."

**The "anything instead of nothing" moment** — [02:45]–[02:58]
A user tests a feature, stops, and is told the input was too short to count — with no output at all. A founder's summary: users expected anything and got nothing, and that's where they're lost.

## Pillar 2 — Consistency

**University of York study (2009)** — [04:54]–[05:19]
Researchers moved a website's navigation bar from left to right partway through a task, changing nothing else. Time on that page more than doubled, and users stayed slower on later pages. Most noticed the change but believed it hadn't slowed them much — so inconsistency costs more than users report or analytics clearly show.

**Quota example** — [05:43]–[06:16]
Commodity: two separate screens saying "You've used all your sessions." Premium: one reusable pattern with a count badge (2 of 2), period copy ("this month"), and the exact reset date.

## Pillar 3 — Failure and recovery

**Service recovery paradox (2007 research review)** — [07:48]–[08:03]
When failures are handled exceptionally well, customers can end up as satisfied as, or more satisfied than, if nothing had gone wrong.

**One bad experience (2018 consumer survey)** — [09:13]
Roughly a third of people say they'll walk away from a product after a single bad experience.

**Chrome offline dinosaur (2014)** — [06:58]
Google replaced a dead offline page with a T-Rex game; about four years later it was being played around 270 million times a month.

**Fintech recovery example** — [08:46]–[08:52]
A financial app replaced a dead-end declined-payment error with an alert naming the diagnostic step (check your balance) and embedding a Contact Support button. Along with related UX changes, this was associated with 60% more new accounts and 79% more money loaded onto cards. Note the "along with related changes" — don't attribute the full lift to the alert alone.

**Cheapest place to buy premium** — [07:35], [09:28]
Because most teams skip failure states and AI tools handle them poorly, they're the lowest-cost place to differentiate.
